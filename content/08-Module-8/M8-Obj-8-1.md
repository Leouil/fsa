# Learning Objective 8.1

## Synthesize the 7-Step Financial Analysis Reporting Framework

**By the end of this note, you can:**
- Explain why a professional report is a *communication* product, not a folder of calculations, and reconstruct the detective-story arc it must follow
- Lay out the seven steps of the reporting architecture and name the course module that supplies the evidence for each one
- Specify what each step must *prove* (not merely cover), and what a reader should be able to conclude after reading it
- Write an executive summary that carries the thesis, the evidence, the risk, and the recommendation — in a single page
- Cross-examine management's MD&A claims against the audited statements and the footnotes, and say which one wins when they disagree

> [!tip] How to read this note
> Read §I–II for the architecture, then work the Sage Inc. case with the model output collapsed (§III), then use §IV's checklist as the pre-submission audit for your own report. In §III and the Review Questions, **attempt each task before expanding a solution** — the answers are collapsed on purpose.

### I. Purpose & Architecture of the Professional Report

#### 1. From calculations to communication

Producing spreadsheets and computing ratios are **intermediate steps**, not the job. The analytical value of a report is created when a set of numbers is turned into an *objective, evidence-backed story* that guides a decision. Two consequences follow, and they are the reason Module 8 exists:

- **A number that no reader can act on is a liability.** A ratio with no interpretation, and no implication for the decision at hand, transfers work back to the reader.
- **A conclusion that no number supports is a liability too.** The report's credibility is spent by its weakest claim; the skeptical reader who catches one unsupported assertion discounts the rest.

The report therefore has one job: **let a decision-maker act without re-deriving your work, and let a skeptic audit every step you took.**

#### 2. The detective-story arc

A professional report connects **economic causes to financial outcomes** in a sequence the reader can follow:

> *Macro conditions and competitive structure* (why the industry earns what it earns) → *management's strategy* (why this firm earns more or less than the industry) → *operating decisions* (how that strategy shows up in margins, working capital, and capital spending) → *accounting quality and risk* (whether the reported numbers can be trusted, and what the statements do not show) → *forward view* (where this leads and what it requires) → *value and decision* (what it is worth to us, and what we do about it).

Note the direction of causation. An analyst who starts with "EBIT fell 12%" and hunts for a reason will produce a report that is a list of symptoms. An analyst who starts with "the industry is consolidating around scale and our position in it is X" produces a report that is an argument.

> [!danger] The three ways a report fails before the numbers do
> 1. **No causal order.** Conclusions arranged in the order the data appeared rather than in the order the reader needs them.
> 2. **No decision.** The report describes the company and stops — the reader is left to supply the recommendation.
> 3. **No audit trail.** Claims without numbers or citations, so a reviewer cannot verify them and cannot reuse them.

#### 3. The critical structural elements

Two elements sit *above* the seven steps, because they are what a senior reader reads first and last:

**The executive summary.** A one-page synthesis carrying: the thesis (one sentence), the supporting evidence (a handful of figures), the recommendation with its valuation or rating, and the principal risks and catalysts. It is written **last** and placed **first** — and it must stand alone, because most readers will read only it.

**The footnote & MD&A cross-examination.** Every professional report is a three-way conversation between the audited statements (what happened), the notes (what the statements do not show), and management's discussion (what management says happened). The analyst's job is to check the third against the first two: leases in the notes against the "asset-light" claim in the MD&A; the maturity schedule against the "no refinancing risk" claim; the segment note against the "diversified" claim. Where the narrative and the filing disagree, **the filing wins** — and the disagreement is itself a finding worth reporting.

### II. The Seven Steps: What Each One Must Prove

Each step has a **question**, **evidence** (with the module that supplies it), and a **proof obligation** — the specific thing the reader must be able to conclude at the end of it. A step that "covers" its topic without discharging the proof obligation is a section of narrative without a function.

#### Step 1 — Industry economic characteristics (where the firm competes)

- **Evidence:** industry structure (Porter's Five Forces), value-chain position, cyclical and macroeconomic dependencies — [[M4-Obj-4-1]].
- **Proof obligation:** the reader can name the **structural forces** that set the industry's profit ceiling, and the one or two that bind hardest for this firm.

#### Step 2 — Corporate strategic positioning (how the firm competes)

- **Evidence:** cost leadership vs. differentiation, regional and product diversity, brand equity and non-financial innovation drivers — [[M4-Obj-4-1]]; peer benchmarking for the relative position — [[M4-Obj-4-2]].
- **Proof obligation:** the reader can state the firm's **position** and its **durability**, and point to the evidence that supports it (margin vs. peers, segment mix, geographic spread).

#### Step 3 — Financial statement examination and quality assessment

- **Evidence:** balance-sheet representation ([[M2-Obj-2-1]]), revenue and expense recognition ([[M2-Obj-2-2]], [[M5-Obj-5-1]]), cash-flow dynamics ([[M2-Obj-2-3]]), earnings quality and normalization ([[M5-Obj-5-3]]).
- **Proof obligation:** the reader knows **which reported figures can be relied upon**, which are adjusted, and why — with the adjustment stated as a number, not an adjective.

#### Step 4 — Present profitability and risk diagnosis

- **Evidence:** common-sizing ([[M3-Obj-3-1]]), trends ([[M3-Obj-3-2]]), Du Pont ROE decomposition ([[M3-Obj-3-3]]), liquidity, coverage, working-capital velocity, operating leverage ([[M6-Obj-6-1]]), distortion and distress models ([[M6-Obj-6-2]]).
- **Proof obligation:** the reader can state the **return** (decomposed into margin, turnover, leverage), the **fragility**, and the single metric that would move first if conditions changed.

#### Step 5 — Pro forma forecasts

- **Evidence:** percentage-of-sales modeling, working-capital assumptions, EFN plug, sensitivity and switch points — [[M7-Obj-7-1]], [[M7-Obj-7-2]].
- **Proof obligation:** the reader knows what the plan **requires** (external funding, margin path, capital intensity) and which assumption the answer is most sensitive to.

#### Step 6 — Business valuation

- **Evidence:** FCFF/WACC DCF, terminal value, enterprise-to-equity bridge, normalized multiples — [[M7-Obj-7-3]].
- **Proof obligation:** the reader has a **value with a stated assumption set and a stated range**, and knows what the market price implies that the model does not.

#### Step 7 — Closing remarks and recommendation

- **Evidence:** the outputs of Steps 1–6, assembled.
- **Proof obligation:** one **explicit, actionable recommendation** (buy / hold / sell; approve / decline / reprice; proceed / defer), with the two or three risks that would invalidate it.

> [!tip] The one-page test
> If your reader could read only your executive summary and act correctly, the report works. Write the summary first as a list of the five claims you would defend, then draft the body to support exactly those five claims. If a body section does not support one of them, cut it or move it to an appendix.

### III. Worked Calculation: The Sage Inc. Initiation Report

> [!tip] Before you flip any answer
> Attempt both tasks before opening the model output. This is a **writing** exercise: the grading is on structure, evidence, and decision, not on prose quality.

#### Scenario

You are an associate analyst writing an **initiation report** on **Sage Inc.**, a multi-line retailer, following its FY2025 annual filing. You compile the following summary indicators from the financial disclosures (\$ thousands):

| Indicator | FY2025 | FY2024 | Note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Net revenue | 215,600 | 153,000 | +40.9% (store rollout) |
| Merchandise inventory | 47,041 | 36,769 | **49.4% of total assets** |
| Cash and securities | 9,333 | 10,386 | **9.8% of total assets** |
| Accounts payable | 14,294 | 7,591 | **+88.3%** — stretched to fund 25 new stores |
| Accounts receivable | 8,960 | 8,350 | |
| Total current assets | 65,846 | 56,264 | |
| Accrued and other current liabilities | 13,167 | 12,841 | |
| Total current liabilities | 27,461 | 20,432 | |
| Cash flow from operations | **+10,024** | −3,767 | rebound |
| Return on equity | 20.5% | — | driven by margin expansion **and** leverage |
| Cash conversion cycle | 107 days | — | |
| Financial leverage index | 1.8× | — | |

**Tasks.** (1) Write a one-paragraph **executive summary**: the core financial opportunity, the primary operational risk, and the decision you recommend. (2) Draft the **analytical outline** — the seven report sections, each with the specific Sage evidence and the module that supplies it.

> [!success]- Solution — model output
> **(1) Executive summary (model):**
> *Sage Inc. delivered \$215.6 million of net sales in FY2025, a 40.9% increase on the prior year, and reported a 20.5% return on equity — but the composition of that return is the story, not its level. Operating cash flow rebounded to +\$10.0 million from a -\$3.8 million deficit, so the growth is at least cash-real at the operating line. The cost of that growth is balance-sheet: merchandise inventory has reached \$47.0 million, 49.4% of total assets, while cash has fallen to 9.8% of assets, and accounts payable — the funding source for the 25-store rollout — has risen 88.3% to \$14.3 million. The expansion is therefore financed by suppliers as much as by the income statement, and a 107-day cash conversion cycle means the cash arrives long after the inventory does. We recommend a **cautious hold**: the operating turnaround is real and funded, but the equity cushion behind it is thinner than a 20.5% ROE suggests, and the plan's next leg depends on inventory turning faster, not on selling more stores.*
>
> **(2) Analytical outline (model):**
> 1. **Industry economic characteristics** — discretionary retail structure, the 25–44 demographic, regional population growth and weather/seasonality ([[M4-Obj-4-1]]).
> 2. **Corporate strategic positioning** — multi-line rollout with a high-margin mix; geographic mitigation of local price competition ([[M4-Obj-4-1]], [[M4-Obj-4-2]]).
> 3. **Accounting & quality examination** — FIFO inventory costing and its effect on the gross margin; leasehold amortization; whether the payables stretch is a policy or an artifact ([[M2-Obj-2-1]], [[M5-Obj-5-1]]).
> 4. **Profitability & risk diagnosis** — common-sizing the two-year balance sheet, three-ratio Du Pont on the 20.5% ROE, the 107-day CCC, and the 1.8× financial leverage index ([[M3-Obj-3-1]], [[M3-Obj-3-3]], [[M6-Obj-6-1]]).
> 5. **Pro forma projections** — three-year percentage-of-sales forecast of the store rollout, the working-capital investment it consumes, and the EFN it requires ([[M7-Obj-7-1]]).
> 6. **Business valuation** — intrinsic DCF on the projected free cash flow and relative EV/EBITDA benchmarking ([[M7-Obj-7-3]]).
> 7. **Final recommendation** — the thesis, the conditions that would change it, and the downside triggers to monitor ([[M7-Obj-7-2]]).

> [!danger] Reading the model output critically
> The model summary makes a judgment ("cautious hold") that the *data* does not force: nothing in the table tells you whether the payables stretch is sustainable, and a 20.5% ROE driven "by margin expansion and leverage" could be either. **A defensible alternative summary would rate the equity on the same evidence — it is the supporting reasoning that must differ, and it must be sourced.** Grade the attempt on whether every claim carries a number, whether the risk is identified with the mechanism that produces it, and whether the recommendation is explicit and falsifiable — not on whether it matches the model.

### IV. The Report Pre-Submission Checklist

Run this against your own draft before you submit. Each line is a question a reviewer will ask.

| # | Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Can the thesis be stated in one sentence? | If not, the report has two theses, and it will read as two reports |
| 2 | Does every number in the executive summary appear in the body with its source? | A summary figure the reader cannot verify is the fastest way to lose them |
| 3 | Does each of the seven sections end with a conclusion, not a description? | A section that ends in "revenue grew 40.9%" has not discharged its proof obligation |
| 4 | Is every claim either a number with a source or a judgment with reasoning? | "Strong management" is not a claim a committee can act on |
| 5 | Have you checked management's MD&A claims against the footnotes? | The cross-examination is the analyst's distinctive contribution |
| 6 | Are the earnings normalized before any multiple or trend is quoted? | Module 5's discipline, applied before the reader applies it for you |
| 7 | Does the forecast disclose its assumptions and its most sensitive one? | Module 7's discipline, carried into the report |
| 8 | Does the valuation quote a range with its assumption set, not a single number? | Module 7's discipline again — and it protects you when the market disagrees |
| 9 | Is the recommendation explicit, and are its invalidation conditions stated? | "It depends" is not a recommendation |
| 10 | Would the report change a reader's mind if the recommendation reversed? | If not, the analysis was never doing work — it was decorating the view |

### Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Financial analysis report** | A decision document that converts numbers into an evidence-backed narrative with an explicit recommendation |
| **Detective-story arc** | The causal ordering of a report: industry → strategy → operations → accounting quality → forward view → value → decision |
| **7-step framework** | Industry → strategy → accounting quality → profitability & risk → pro forma forecast → valuation → closing recommendation |
| **Proof obligation** | The specific conclusion a section must leave the reader able to draw |
| **Executive summary** | One standalone page carrying thesis, evidence, recommendation, and principal risks |
| **MD&A cross-examination** | Checking management's narrative against the audited statements and the notes; the filing wins |
| **Analytical outline** | The map of a report: sections, the evidence in each, and the conclusion each must deliver |
| **Falsifiable recommendation** | A recommendation stated so that specific observable conditions would change it |
| **Appendix discipline** | Support that a reader may want but that would distract from the argument; it belongs behind the argument, not inside it |

---

> [!danger] Red Flags — Report Writing
> - A summary that describes rather than concludes
> - Numbers without sources, or sources without numbers
> - A report organised by document (balance sheet, income statement, cash flow) instead of by argument
> - Management's framing repeated verbatim as the analyst's conclusion
> - A recommendation with no stated invalidation conditions
> - A single-point valuation quoted without its assumption set
> - Multiples applied to unnormalized earnings
> - Forecasts without a disclosed assumption list
> - An appendix that contains the argument the body should have made
> - A report written for the author rather than the reader who must decide

### Review Questions

> [!question]- Q1: What is the difference between a report that "covers" the seven steps and one that discharges their proof obligations?
> Covering means the topic appears — a paragraph on industry structure, a table of ratios. Discharging means the reader is left able to conclude the thing the step exists to establish: which structural force binds hardest, which reported figures can be relied upon, which metric moves first, what the plan requires, what the cash flows are worth, and what to do about it. A report can cover all seven topics and discharge none of the obligations, in which case it is a well-organised file rather than a decision document.

> [!question]- Q2: Why must the executive summary be written last but placed first?
> Written last, because it can only summarise claims the body actually supports and numbers the body actually derives — writing it first forces you to revise the thesis to match whatever the analysis later shows. Placed first, because that is how senior readers read: they take the page, decide whether to continue, and often do not. It must also stand alone: no claim in it may depend on a page the reader has not reached.

> [!question]- Q3: In the MD&A cross-examination, management's discussion and the footnotes disagree. Which wins, and why?
> The filing wins, always — the notes and audited statements are the verified record, and the MD&A is a narrative overlay that is not separately audited. The disagreement is itself the finding: a management narrative that cannot be reconciled to the notes is a governance and reporting-quality signal worth reporting in Step 3, not a drafting detail to smooth over.

> [!question]- Q4: Sage's 20.5% ROE is "driven by margin expansion and leverage." Why should a report not simply repeat that description?
> Because it is management's characterisation of a ratio the reader is being asked to interpret. The analyst's job is to decompose it — margin, asset turnover, and the 1.8× leverage multiplier (Du Pont, [[M3-Obj-3-3]]) — and to say whether the operating improvement or the balance sheet produced the return. Sage's own numbers say the leverage multiplier rose: equity is being levered by payables that grew 88.3%, so an ROE that looks like operating success may be a function of supplier funding.

> [!question]- Q5: What does the checklist's tenth line — "would the report change a reader's mind if the recommendation reversed?" — test for?
> It tests whether the analysis is doing real work. A report that would read identically whatever the recommendation (because the body is a description of the company rather than an argument) is decoration. If the reader's mind cannot change on the evidence, then no evidence in the report is load-bearing — and a reader who notices this will discount everything else.

### Practice Activity

> [!tip] Warm-up path
> New to the material? Do task 1 with the §IV checklist open; tasks 2–4 belong after the Module 8 quiz.

You are writing the initiation report on **Sage Inc.** from the §III data pack:

1. **Draft the thesis sentence.** One sentence, no numbers. Then list the five claims your report must support. (Do not write the body yet.)
2. **Build the outline.** For each of the seven steps, name the specific Sage figure you would use and the lecture module that supplies it. Flag any step where the current data pack is too thin — that is a research gap you must disclose.
3. **Write the executive summary.** One paragraph: thesis, three supporting figures, the primary risk with its mechanism, and an explicit recommendation with its invalidation condition.
4. **Cross-examine.** Management's framing in the pack says the ROE was "driven by margin expansion and financial leverage." Using only the given figures, decompose the risk: which balance-sheet movement would have to reverse for the ROE to fall, and what would that do to the CFO rebound? Answer in three sentences.
5. **Redraft as a credit memo.** Keep the same evidence, change the audience to a lender (per [[M8-Obj-8-2]] §I). In one sentence, state what you would lead with instead of the 40.9% revenue growth.
